# Elicitation Prompts Reference

Five guided prompts for gathering writing samples when the user has insufficient
existing material. Present these sequentially, with one concrete observation after
each response to sustain engagement.

---

## When to Use Elicitation

Use the full elicitation sequence when the user has fewer than 150 words of
existing samples. If they have 150-500 words, use 1-2 targeted prompts to fill
dimensional gaps. If they have 500+ words, proceed directly to analysis.

**Minimum viable sample sizes:**
- 150 words: enough for a draft profile with low confidence
- 500 words: enough for a standard profile
- 1500+ words: enough for a high-confidence profile

Two or three well-chosen prompts from different categories typically yield 500-700 words.

---

## The Progressive Reward Approach

After each prompt response, share one concrete, specific observation before
moving to the next prompt. This rewards participation and builds trust in the
analysis. The observation should be genuinely specific — not "you write clearly"
but "you opened with a question and then answered it immediately, which I'm already
seeing as a pattern."

This also surfaces early signals the user can confirm or correct, improving
profile accuracy before the full metavoice synthesis.

---

## Prompt 1: Explanation

**Present this prompt exactly:**

> Pick something you know well — a concept, a process, a hobby — and explain
> it to someone encountering it for the first time. Write 2-4 paragraphs.

**What it reveals:**
- Vocabulary depth and domain register
- Formality level when explaining to a stranger
- Whether the writer uses analogies, examples, or abstract principles
- Pedagogical patience (or lack of it)

**What to look for:**
- Does the writer start with a definition, a story, or a question?
- Concrete analogies vs. abstract principles?
- Second-person ("you'll notice") or third-person distancing?

**Dimensional weight:** Strong for Lexical and Tone. Moderate for Syntactic.

---

## Prompt 2: Persuasion

**Present this prompt exactly:**

> Take a position on something you feel strongly about — in your field, in life,
> wherever. Argue for it. What would you say to someone who disagrees? Write 2-4 paragraphs.

**What it reveals:**
- Rhetorical strategy under conviction (evidence-first vs. emotion-first)
- How the writer handles disagreement
- Sentence complexity when arguing
- Use of qualifiers vs. declarative certainty

**What to look for:**
- Concession before argument? ("I understand why someone might think X, but...")
- Structured (claim-evidence-implication) or discursive?
- Word choice: hedged ("might", "could") or declarative ("is", "will")?

**Dimensional weight:** Strong for Tone, Syntactic, and Micro-patterns. This prompt
often surfaces the most distinctive voice characteristics because conviction drops
the writer's performative register.

---

## Prompt 3: Narrative

**Present this prompt exactly:**

> Tell me about a time you learned something the hard way. Include enough detail
> that I can picture it. Write 2-4 paragraphs.

**What it reveals:**
- Intimacy ceiling — how close the writer lets the reader get
- Detail density and specificity
- Vulnerability vs. polish
- Humor tendencies and temporal structure

**What to look for:**
- Sensory and situational detail: vivid or abstract?
- Sentence length shifts in tense moments?
- Editorializing ("looking back, I realize...")?
- How is vulnerability handled?

**Dimensional weight:** Strong for Tone, Micro-patterns, and Syntactic (narrative
often shows rhythm variation most clearly).

---

## Prompt 4: Compression

**Present this prompt exactly:**

> Summarize what you do for a living — or your biggest current project — in exactly
> 2-3 sentences.

**What it reveals:**
- Which details survive compression
- Sentence structure under constraint
- Abstraction vs. specificity when space is limited
- Self-presentation voice

**What to look for:**
- Clause density: one long sentence or short declaratives?
- Jargon (insider reader) or plain language (outsider)?
- Active or passive construction?

**Dimensional weight:** High signal per word for Syntactic and Lexical. Weight lightly
for Tone and Punctuation (too short to reliably surface those patterns).

---

## Prompt 5: Critique

**Present this prompt exactly:**

> Pick something in your field that most people get wrong, or that frustrates you.
> What's wrong with it and what would you do instead? Write 2-4 paragraphs.

**What it reveals:**
- Directness vs. diplomacy when criticizing
- "Perhaps/might/could" vs. "is wrong/fails/misses the point"
- How alternatives are positioned: prescribe or suggest?
- Critical voice register: frustrated? Clinical? Amused?

**What to look for:**
- Names the target or hedges with "some people"?
- Vocabulary of critique: "fails" vs. "falls short" vs. "is problematic"
- Punctuation under frustration: more dashes? More rhetorical questions?

**Dimensional weight:** Strong for Tone, Micro-patterns, and Punctuation. If the
writer softens significantly in critique, that is itself a strong voice signal.

---

## Notes on Prompt Sequencing

The prompts are ordered safe-to-personal: explanation and persuasion feel professional,
narrative requires more trust, compression is brief and low-stakes, critique rounds
out with a professional confidence signal.

If a user balks at any prompt, skip it. Note the gap in the profile and mark
affected dimensions as lower confidence.

For users with existing samples: load those first, run the script, and use the
resulting dimensional gaps to select 1-2 targeted prompts rather than the full sequence.
